# Slide Deck Creator — Evidence Base

**Skill file:** `plugins/skills/planning/slide-deck/SKILL.md`
**Category:** Planning

## What the skill does
Turns a lesson plan (or standalone topic) into a slide-by-slide presentation outline with speaker notes, defaulting to minimal on-screen text — "prompts, visuals, and anchors" that support the teacher's voice rather than replace it. The teacher chooses a slide style (Minimal, Guided, or Text-heavy) up front, and the skill applies it consistently, reserving dense text for slides students genuinely need to read from.

## Evidence base

### Managing cognitive load optimises learning
- **Publisher/author:** Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO)
- **Year:** Published 18 September 2023, last updated 30 April 2025
- **URL:** https://www.edresearch.edu.au/summaries-explainers/explainers/managing-cognitive-load-optimises-learning
- **Australian source:** Yes
- **What it says:** Explains cognitive overload (when students struggle to process and store new information in working memory) and sets out teaching strategies to manage it — breaking content into small chunks with well-defined goals, providing worked examples/models, and avoiding unstructured approaches for novices. Draws on Sweller, Kirschner, Clark, Rosenshine, and Fiorella & Mayer.
- **Relevance to this skill:** Directly backs the skill's central design premise — that slides should carry "headings, key terms, steps, and sentence starters" rather than dense text — because unmanaged extraneous content on a slide competes with working memory capacity for the actual lesson content.

### Cognitive load theory: Research that teachers really need to understand
- **Publisher/author:** Centre for Education Statistics and Evaluation (CESE), NSW Department of Education
- **Year:** 2017
- **URL:** https://education.nsw.gov.au/about-us/education-data-and-research/cese/publications/literature-reviews/cognitive-load-theory
- **Australian source:** Yes
- **What it says:** Explains that working memory can hold only about four "chunks" of information at a time, and that overloading it (e.g. with unnecessary text or detail) impairs learning; explicit guidance with practice and feedback outperforms discovery approaches for novices. Notes Dylan Wiliam has called cognitive load theory "the single most important thing for teachers to know."
- **Relevance to this skill:** Gives the specific mechanism (working memory's ~4-chunk limit) behind the skill's rule "One idea per slide — ruthlessly" and its default toward Minimal slide style — a text-heavy slide plus a talking teacher creates two simultaneous demands on the same limited channel.

### Emeritus Professor John Sweller — Cognitive Load Theory
- **Publisher/author:** UNSW Sydney (UNSW Research profile)
- **Year:** Ongoing body of work; profile current as of 2026
- **URL:** https://research.unsw.edu.au/people/emeritus-professor-john-sweller
- **Australian source:** Yes (Sweller developed cognitive load theory at UNSW Sydney)
- **What it says:** Confirms Sweller, based at UNSW Sydney, developed cognitive load theory — human cognitive architecture and evolutionary psychology applied to instructional design — and that it is "one of the most highly cited educational psychology theories," tested through randomised controlled trials of its instructional consequences.
- **Relevance to this skill:** Establishes that the theory underlying the skill's minimal-text design principle originates from an Australian researcher and institution, strengthening the "trusted, local evidence" framing for an Australian teacher audience, not just an imported US framework.

### Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning (Coherence Principle)
- **Publisher/author:** Richard E. Mayer and Logan Fiorella, in *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (Cambridge University Press)
- **Year:** 2014
- **URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E
- **Australian source:** No (international — Mayer's coherence principle, part of his widely-cited Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning)
- **What it says:** States the coherence principle — people learn more deeply from a multimedia presentation when extraneous material (irrelevant text, images, or decoration) is excluded rather than included. Supported in 23 of 23 experimental tests, with a median effect size of 0.86.
- **Relevance to this skill:** This is the closest direct evidence for the skill's specific medium (slides/multimedia presentations) rather than teaching in general — it backs the instruction that "Large text blocks only appear when students genuinely need to read from the slide" and the principle that speaker notes, not slide text, should carry explanation.

## How this evidence should sharpen the skill
1. **Name the theory once, briefly, in the intro.** The opening paragraph already states the design philosophy ("Good lesson slides are not documents put on a screen...") but doesn't say why. A single parenthetical after that paragraph — e.g. "(this follows cognitive load theory and Mayer's coherence principle: extraneous on-screen text competes with the teacher's spoken explanation for the same limited working memory)" — would let a sceptical teacher or school leader see the design choice is evidence-grounded, not a stylistic preference.
2. **Strengthen the case against Text-heavy as a default.** The skill already warns "Reserve for slides students genuinely need to read from — not as a default," but given the coherence principle's strong effect size (0.86 across 23/23 tests), consider adding one explicit sentence to Step 4's planning question: "Note: even text-heavy decks should isolate the passage/excerpt on its own slide rather than mixing it with other instructional content" — this reflects Mayer's finding that it's not text per se that hurts learning, but *extraneous* material mixed with essential content.
3. **Tie "one slide per key teaching point" to chunking.** Step 5 and the Principles section both implicitly chunk content one-idea-per-slide, but the underlying CESE/AERO rationale (working memory holds ~4 chunks) is never surfaced. A short line under "Principles" — "Chunking supports working memory: one idea per slide, not one slide per fact" — would make the "One idea per slide — ruthlessly" principle traceable to a named mechanism rather than reading as an unexplained style rule.
4. **Differentiation slide callouts already align with AERO's scaffolding guidance** (worked examples, sentence starters) but the skill could explicitly note that these differentiation supports should be *faded* across a unit rather than repeated identically every lesson — AERO's guidance on scaffolding stresses gradual removal of support as students gain proficiency. This is a smaller, secondary suggestion since slide-deck operates lesson-by-lesson rather than across a unit.

## Caveats / contested evidence
Cognitive load theory and the coherence principle are both well-established with strong replication (Mayer's coherence tests show 23/23 positive results), but most of the underlying experiments are conducted in relatively controlled, short-duration learning tasks (e.g. a single multimedia lesson in a lab or short classroom trial) rather than long-run studies of whole lesson sequences delivered via slides in real classrooms. The skill's specific three-way style split (Minimal/Guided/Text-heavy) is a reasonable operationalisation of the research but is not itself a tested taxonomy from the literature — it's the skill's own design translation of the coherence and cognitive load principles into a teacher-facing choice.
