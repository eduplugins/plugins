# Differentiation Suggester — Evidence Base

**Skill file:** `plugins/skills/differentiation/suggester/SKILL.md`
**Category:** Differentiation

## What the skill does
Generates a curriculum-grounded menu of differentiation options — for support, extension, English language learners (EAL/D/EAL/ELL), and specific learning needs — before a lesson or unit is planned, organised around adjusting the input, task, and output of a lesson. It offers UDL or Explicit Teaching as optional lenses to revisit the menu afterward.

## Evidence base

### Australian Professional Standards for Teachers — Standard 1.5 and "In the Classroom: Differentiation"
- **Publisher/author:** AITSL (Australian Institute for Teaching and School Leadership)
- **Year:** Standards published 2011, revised 2018; illustration-of-practice resource page current
- **URL:** https://www.aitsl.edu.au/teach/improve-practice/in-the-classroom/differentiation
- **Australian source:** Yes
- **What it says:** Standard 1.5 requires teachers to differentiate teaching to meet the specific learning needs of students across the full range of abilities; AITSL illustrates this through a worked classroom example where a lead teacher pre-assesses students, groups by readiness, and adjusts task complexity per group while holding the same learning intention.
- **Relevance to this skill:** This is the professional standard the suggester is designed to help teachers meet proactively — "designed in rather than retrofitted" (per the skill's own description) is the practical difference between reactive Standard 1.5 compliance and the anticipatory design the AITSL illustration models.

### English as an Additional Language or Dialect (EAL/D) students
- **Publisher/author:** ACARA (Australian Curriculum, Assessment and Reporting Authority)
- **Year:** 2022 (Australian Curriculum Version 9)
- **URL:** https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/student-diversity/eal-d-students
- **Australian source:** Yes
- **What it says:** EAL/D students have the same cognitive capacity as their peers but need explicit, level-appropriate language instruction and modified ways to demonstrate understanding (e.g. a Beginning-phase student showing understanding of a science concept via a diagram rather than written text); basic interpersonal English typically develops in 2–3 years, but academic language proficiency takes 5–7 years.
- **Relevance to this skill:** Directly backs the "[EAL/D / EAL / ELL] learners" section (vocabulary support, task access, participation) — the skill's suggestion to "accept home-language responses" and offer "a visual model or worked example" mirrors ACARA's own worked example of alternative-format output for Beginning-phase learners.

### The UDL Guidelines
- **Publisher/author:** CAST (Center for Applied Special Technology)
- **Year:** Version 3.0, 2024
- **URL:** https://udlguidelines.cast.org/
- **Australian source:** No — international framework, but widely referenced by Australian education authorities including AITSL
- **What it says:** UDL organises design flexibility around three principles: multiple means of Engagement, Representation, and Action & Expression, so learner variability is planned for from the start.
- **Relevance to this skill:** CAST's three principles map almost exactly onto the skill's own "Adjusting the input / Adjusting the task / Adjusting the output" structure (Step 2) — Representation ≈ input, Engagement ≈ task design, Action & Expression ≈ output. This is a strong structural match worth naming explicitly, not just offering as an optional lens afterward.

### Teach explicitly (practice guide)
- **Publisher/author:** AERO (Australian Education Research Organisation)
- **Year:** February 2024
- **URL:** https://www.edresearch.edu.au/guides-resources/practice-guides/teach-explicitly
- **Australian source:** Yes
- **What it says:** Explicit teaching — explaining, demonstrating and modelling content, then releasing responsibility gradually ("I do, we do, you do") — reduces cognitive overload for students still building foundational knowledge, and should precede more complex, less-guided tasks.
- **Relevance to this skill:** Backs the "Explicit Teaching" framework offer in Step 3 and the cognitive-load reasoning implicit in several support suggestions (chunking tasks into steps with checkpoints, partially completed graphic organisers, sentence frames) — these are explicit-teaching scaffolds, not generic "extra help."

## How this evidence should sharpen the skill
1. **Name CAST's three principles in the "Support" section headers.** Step 2 already splits support into "input / task / output," which is functionally UDL's Representation / Engagement / Action & Expression. Making that mapping explicit (a one-line note before the Support section) would let a teacher who already knows UDL recognise the structure immediately, and would justify the UDL framework offer in Step 3 as a genuine deepening rather than a new lens.
2. **Ground the EAL/D timeframe (2–3 years conversational vs 5–7 years academic English) in the suggestions themselves.** Currently "Adjusting the input" and "EAL/D learners" sections list strategies without distinguishing by proficiency phase. Given ACARA's own worked example differentiates by phase (Beginning/Emerging vs Developing/Consolidating), the skill could ask the teacher's EAL/D students' approximate phase in Step 1's planning questions and tailor suggestions accordingly, rather than offering one undifferentiated EAL/D menu.
3. **Tie "chunking" and "sentence frame" suggestions explicitly to cognitive load reduction.** AERO's Teach Explicitly guide frames chunking and scaffolds as cognitive-load management, not just "extra support." A one-line rationale added to the Support section (e.g., "these reduce the number of new demands held in working memory at once") would strengthen the skill's own stated principle that "support reduces the barrier, not the expectation" with a named mechanism.
4. **Clarify that "Explicit Teaching" and "UDL" are not competing choices.** The current framework offer (Step 3) presents them as parallel alternative lenses a teacher picks between. Since AERO frames explicit teaching as what happens *before* more open, UDL-flexible tasks (foundational skills first, then choice and flexibility), the offer text could note they are sequential/complementary rather than alternative, so teachers don't read this as "pick a philosophy."

## Caveats / contested evidence
None identified as contested for the Support/Extension/EAL/D structure itself. One caveat worth surfacing: AERO's evidence base leans toward structured, teacher-directed approaches for skill acquisition, while UDL leans toward learner agency and choice; the skill currently offers both as if interchangeable "lenses," when in practice a teacher applying an AERO-style explicit-teaching read might tighten choice and scaffolding exactly where a UDL-style read would loosen it for the same student. The skill's soft-offer wording already avoids over-claiming either framework is definitive — worth keeping that framing rather than presenting either as the single "correct" answer.
